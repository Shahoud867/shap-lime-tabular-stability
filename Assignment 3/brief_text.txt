Overview
Every group submits one publishable-quality research paper written in IEEE LaTeX format, by
Saturday, 21 November 2026, 11:59 PM, together with its analysis files and a live presentation
(demo). The assignment carries 100 marks and is the foundation of your semester project.
You choose one of three paper types: a Systematic Literature Review (SLR), a Survey (review) paper,
or an Empirical paper. The paper type changes how you build your argument, but every group still has
to show a research gap, a novel contribution, its own analysis, results and a demo.
Rules that apply to every group:
    •    Base paper: a Q1/Q2 journal paper published in 2025 or 2026. Conference papers, arXiv/SSRN
         and other preprints are not accepted as the base paper. Verify the quartile on SCImago (SJR) or
         Web of Science JCR and keep a screenshot.
    •    Other references: papers from the last 5 years (2021–2026) may be cited in the Introduction,
         Literature Review and Discussion. Older works only if they are foundational (e.g., the original
         paper of a theory you build on, such as the Technology Acceptance Model or the Theory of
         Planned Behaviour).
    •    Data: test your model on your own data plus at least one more relevant sample or dataset.
         Where the base paper's data is publicly available, use it as well. SLR/Survey groups: your
         literature corpus counts as your dataset (see Tracks A and B).
    •    Analysis tools: any suitable statistical or qualitative tool (e.g., SPSS, SmartPLS, AMOS, R,
         Python, Stata, NVivo). Script-based tools are preferred (see Section 6.3). The paper must be
         written in LaTeX on Overleaf with the IEEE template; Word or other formats are not accepted.
    •    Integrity: plagiarism or AI-generated content above 15% = zero marks. No deadline extension.
The goal is to move beyond replication: read critically, find what is missing, and contribute something
new that a journal or conference reviewer would accept.

Requirements Common to Every Paper

Whatever the track, a reviewer looks for three things first: a clear contribution in the Introduction, a
gap-based literature table, and professional figures. These are mandatory.

(a) Novelty and contributions at the end of the Introduction
The last part of the Introduction must state, in this order:
    1. Research gap: 2–3 sentences on what the base paper and recent literature leave unsolved.
    2. Novelty: one short paragraph on what is new in your work and how it differs from the base
       paper.
    3. Contributions: a numbered list of 3–5 specific, verifiable contributions. Each starts with a verb
       and, where possible, a number.
    4. Paper organisation: one paragraph: "The rest of this paper is organised as follows. Section II"

Track A: Systematic Literature Review (SLR)
An SLR answers 3–5 focused research questions using a protocol that another researcher could repeat
and get the same set of papers. Follow the Tranfield, Denyer and Smart (2003) guidelines for systematic
reviews in management research and report screening with the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram and
checklist.

Required Method Steps

    1. Research questions (RQs): 3–5 RQs, framed with PICOC (Population, Intervention,
       Comparison, Outcome, Context). Example: RQ1: Which theories are used to explain fintech
       adoption among SMEs (2021–2026)? RQ2: Which research designs, samples and measures are
       used to test them?
    2. Search strategy: at least 4 databases (e.g., Scopus, Web of Science, EBSCO Business Source
       Complete, ScienceDirect, Emerald Insight, SpringerLink, JSTOR, ProQuest). Report the exact
       Boolean search string per database, the date searched and hit counts.
    3. Inclusion/Exclusion criteria (IC/EC): a table, e.g., IC1 peer-reviewed journal or conference,
       IC2 published 2021–2026, EC1 non-English, EC2 no empirical evidence, EC3 short papers
       under 4 pages.
    4. Screening: title/abstract → full text → snowballing (backward and forward). At least two
       members screen independently; report agreement with Cohen's kappa.
    5. Quality assessment (QA): 5–8 QA questions scored 0 / 0.5 / 1; drop studies below a stated
       threshold.
    6. Data extraction: a form (spreadsheet) with fields such as year, journal, theory,
       country/industry, research design, sample size, measures, key findings, limitations. Submit it
       as a supplementary file.
    7. Synthesis: quantitative (counts, trends, meta-analysis where effect sizes are comparable) and
       qualitative (thematic analysis) answers to each RQ.

Analysis Component (counts as Part B)

    •    Build a bibliometric analysis of your exported corpus (Scopus/WoS CSV or BibTeX), e.g.,
         with VOSviewer, Bibliometrix (R) or Python: publication trend per year, top journals, keyword
         co-occurrence network, author collaboration, and topic modelling (LDA or BERTopic).
    •    Sensitivity analysis: show how findings change when you vary the protocol, e.g., removing
         one database, tightening the QA threshold, or excluding snowballed papers. Report which
         conclusions are robust.
    •    Pilot validation: test the framework your SLR proposes with a small pilot study (e.g., 5–8
         expert interviews or a pilot survey of at least 30 respondents), and compare the outcome with
         the base paper's findings.

Paper Outline (IEEE)

    1. Title, Abstract (200–250 words), Index Terms (4–6 keywords)
    2. Introduction: motivation, why an SLR is needed now, comparison table with existing reviews
       (including the base paper), list of contributions, paper organisation
    3. Theoretical Background: key concepts and theories relevant to the RQs (keep it to 1 page)
    4. Research Methodology: RQs, search strategy, IC/EC table, PRISMA flow diagram, QA
       criteria, extraction form, threats-to-validity plan
    5. Bibliometric Results: trend plots, journal/keyword/topic analyses (400 DPI)
    6. Results by Research Question: one subsection per RQ with tables and figures
    7. Proposed Framework / Taxonomy: your synthesised conceptual framework with its diagram
       and numbered propositions, plus pilot validation results vs. the base paper
    8. Practical Implications and Case Study: where the framework applies; one detailed case study
    9. Sensitivity Analysis: robustness of findings to protocol changes
    10. Research Gaps and Future Directions: gap table: gap, evidence (which studies), proposed
        direction
    11. Threats to Validity: construct, internal, external, conclusion validity
    12. Conclusion
    13. References (IEEE style)


Minimum figures/tables: PRISMA diagram, IC/EC table, QA table, extraction summary table, 3+
bibliometric plots, gap table, framework diagram, propositions table.


Track B: Survey Paper
A survey (review) paper organises a whole area into an original taxonomy, compares approaches
critically, and sets out open challenges. Unlike an SLR, the search need not be fully protocol-driven,
but you must state your scope and how papers were selected. A survey that only summarises papers one
by one will be marked as a literature review, not a survey.

What Makes It Publishable

    •    Novel taxonomy: your own classification of the area (e.g., by theory, level of analysis, research
         design, industry or region), drawn as a hierarchical figure. It must differ from taxonomies in
         existing reviews and you must say how.
    •    Comparison with existing reviews: a table of 6–10 recent review papers (2021–2026) against

         criteria such as scope, years covered, taxonomy, empirical comparison, future directions. Your
         survey must tick boxes the others miss.
    •    Critical comparative tables: for each taxonomy branch, compare studies on theory, context,
         research design, sample, measures, key findings and limitations.
    •    Lessons learned at the end of each major section, and a consolidated open challenges and
         future directions section.

Analysis Component (counts as Part B)

    •    Comparative analysis: test 3 or more competing models or approaches from different branches
         of your taxonomy on the same dataset, using the same criteria. Examples: TAM vs. UTAUT
         vs. TPB on one survey dataset (R², Q², model fit); three measures of firm performance on one
         secondary dataset; pooled OLS vs. fixed-effects vs. random-effects on one panel dataset.
    •    Proposed improvement: a small, clearly novel extension of the best-performing model (e.g.,
         an added construct, mediator or moderator), with a robustness analysis of each added
         component.
    •    Bibliometric support: publication trend and keyword map of your corpus to justify the
         taxonomy.

Paper Outline (IEEE)

    1. Title, Abstract, Index Terms
    2. Introduction: motivation, scope, comparison table with existing reviews (base paper
       included), contributions, organisation figure
    3. Survey Methodology: databases, keywords, period, selection criteria, number of papers (a
       short PRISMA-style figure is recommended)
    4. Theoretical Background: concepts and theories needed for the taxonomy
    5. Proposed Taxonomy: taxonomy figure and explanation of each dimension
    6. Review by Taxonomy Category: one section per branch with comparison tables and lessons
       learned
    7. Comparative Analysis and Proposed Extension: setup, research-design diagram, model
       equations, results vs. base paper (400 DPI)
    8. Robustness / Sensitivity Analysis
    9. Practical Implications and Case Study
    10. Open Challenges and Future Research Directions
    11. Conclusion
    12. References (60–100, IEEE style)


Minimum figures/tables: taxonomy figure, existing-review comparison table, 1 comparison table per
taxonomy branch, comparative results tables + plots, robustness table, research-design diagram,
challenges table.


Track C: Empirical Study
An empirical paper shows, with your own data, that an extended model improves on a 2025/2026 Q1/Q2
base paper. Quantitative or mixed-methods designs are accepted. The paper must contain both Part A
(research) and Part B (analysis) of the original assignment.

Required Work

    1. Replicate the base paper's model: adapt its measurement instrument (with citations for every
       scale), collect your own data and estimate the same model. Report your results next to the
       published ones and explain any difference (context, sample, time period, estimation method).
    2. Identify gaps in the base paper and in the literature review: a gap table (gap → evidence →
       how you address it).
    3. Propose an extended model that addresses at least one gap. Novelty must be real: a new
       construct, mediator or moderator, a new theoretical combination, or a new method, not only re-
       running the same model in a different country or sample.
    4. Evaluate base vs. extended model on your main sample and at least one additional sample or
       dataset (e.g., a second respondent group, a secondary dataset, or the base paper's public data),
       using the same criteria (reliability, validity, path coefficients, R², model fit) plus any new ones
       you justify.
    5. Robustness analysis: remove or change each added component of your model one at a time
       (nested model comparison) and report the effect in a table and a plot. Add at least one alternative
       specification or subsample check.
    6. Statistical rigour: justify the sample size (e.g., an a priori power analysis in G*Power); report
       reliability (Cronbach's α, composite reliability), validity (AVE, HTMT) and a common method
       bias check; use bootstrapped confidence intervals (e.g., 5,000 resamples) for indirect effects;
       state the significance level used.
    7. Research ethics: informed consent, anonymity and voluntary participation for all primary data;
       keep the consent form for submission.
    8. Practical implications and a detailed case study in a real business setting (an organisation,
       sector or management decision).


Paper Outline (IEEE)

    1. Title, Abstract (150–250 words), Index Terms
    2. Introduction: problem, motivation, base paper summary with citation, research gap, numbered
       contributions, paper organisation
    3. Literature Review and Hypotheses Development: grouped by theme, each hypothesis
       justified, ending in a comparison table of 10–15 recent studies and the gaps they leave
    4. Theoretical Background: underpinning theory and a construct definitions table
    5. Conceptual Model and Research Design: conceptual model diagram, research-design
       flowchart, numbered hypotheses (H1, H2, …), model equations, sampling, instrument (source
       of each scale) and data collection procedure
    6. Base vs. Extended Model Comparison: a side-by-side table of constructs, logic and research
       design
    7. Data and Analysis Setup: sample profile table, data screening (missing values, outliers,
       normality), software and version, estimation settings, evaluation criteria with formulas and
       thresholds
    8. Results and Discussion: measurement model, structural model, and tables and 400 DPI plots
       comparing base vs. extended model on every sample; explain why the results come out as they
       do
    9. Robustness Analysis: table + plot of each model variant

IEEE LaTeX Format
All papers use the official IEEEtran class on Overleaf: \documentclass[journal]{IEEEtran} for
Survey/SLR papers aimed at journals, or \documentclass[conference]{IEEEtran} for Track C papers.
On Overleaf, start from New Project →Templates → "IEEE Bare Demo Template for
Journals/Conferences". Do not change margins, fonts or column layout.
Formatting rules:
    •    Figures: vector (PDF/EPS) or PNG at 400 DPI; axis labels and legends readable at column
         width; caption below the figure. Use figure* for full-width figures.
    •    Tables: caption above the table; use booktabs (\toprule, \midrule, \bottomrule), no vertical
         lines; bold the best result.
    •    Equations: numbered with equation/align; define every symbol once; keep a notation table if
         there are more than 10 symbols.
    •    Hypotheses and propositions: numbered (H1, H2, … or P1, P2, …), each stated once in the

    •    Cross-referencing: every figure, table and equation has a \label{} and is referred to in the

    •    References: BibTeX only (references.bib), IEEE numbered style [1]. Export BibTeX from the

    •    Writing style: formal, third person or "we", past tense for what you did, present tense for what

Academic Integrity, Referencing and AI Use
Any report with plagiarism or AI-generated content above 15% receives zero marks, for the whole
group. Run your own check before submitting and attach the report.
    •    Similarity check: Turnitin (or the tool the department provides). Exclude the bibliography
         only; everything else counts.
    •    Write in your own words. Paraphrasing a paper sentence by sentence is still plagiarism. Read,
         close the paper, then write what you understood, and cite it.
    •    Cite everything you use: ideas, figures, datasets, questionnaire scales, code and equations. A
         figure adapted from a paper needs "Adapted from [n]" in the caption.
    •    AI tools: may be used only for grammar checks, LaTeX debugging and code debugging. They
         must not write sections of the paper, invent references or generate results. Declare any use in
         an Acknowledgment line.
    •    Fake or unverifiable references (a common AI error) are treated as fabrication: zero marks.
         Every reference must have a working DOI or publisher link.
    •    Results must come from your own data and analysis. Copying numbers from another paper
         as your own results, or inventing or altering survey responses, is fabrication.
    •    Contribution statement: add a short CRediT-style paragraph at the end of the paper stating
         what each member did. Members unable to explain their part in the demo may be marked
         individually.
