\section{Problem and research questions}
\textbf{Problem (unchanged from Assignment 1 \cite{a1proposal}).} In tabular, high-stakes classification such as fraud detection and credit scoring, practitioners treat a prediction that barely changes under a small input change as a sign that its SHAP \cite{lundberg2017} or LIME \cite{ribeiro2016} explanation is also reliable. Whether this inference holds for linear and tree-ensemble classifiers has not been tested with a controlled design. This review establishes what the literature already shows before the empirical study is built. We use PICOC rather than PICO because the context, tabular data in high-stakes business settings, is part of the problem itself; the Context element is what limits the search to tabular models (Table~\ref{tab:picoc}).

\vspace{-6pt}
\textbf{RQ1 (Shahoud).} How do studies measure the stability of SHAP and LIME explanations for tabular models, and under which perturbation or resampling regimes? Key terms: stability metric, perturbation regime, prediction-preserving and noise floor. This question identifies defensible metrics and a perturbation protocol.

\textbf{RQ2 (Saif).} What evidence shows whether stable or accurate predictions indicate stable SHAP and LIME explanations, and when does that link break? Key terms: prediction change, explanation drift and decoupling. This tests the assumption linking prediction reliability to explanation reliability.

\textbf{RQ3 (joint).} Which mechanisms (split thresholds, bin edges, decision boundaries, collinearity, sampling) explain SHAP and LIME instability on tabular models, and can instability be predicted without running perturbations? Key terms: split threshold, bin edge, decision boundary, fragility score and uncertainty score. This explains why explanations change and assesses possible warning scores.

{\small RQ1 and RQ2 are unchanged. RQ3 follows the refined questions agreed in the project strategy review: the earlier ``drivers and remedies'' question was replaced by the mechanism and fragility-score question, and a logged supplementary search was run for it (Sections~\ref{sec:sources}--\ref{sec:search}).
\textbf{Key terms.} \textit{Split threshold}: the cut-point at which a tree node sends an instance left or right. \textit{Bin edge}: a boundary of LIME's discretisation of a continuous feature. \textit{Fragility score}: a per-instance score, computed from the model alone, that predicts how much an explanation will change. \textit{Stability}: how similar the explanations of the same instance stay across repeated runs, retraining or small input changes. \textit{Perturbation regime}: what is varied (explainer sampling, model refits or input values). \textit{Prediction-preserving}: a perturbation that leaves the predicted class, and nearly the predicted probability, unchanged. \textit{Noise floor}: the change between repeated explainer calls when nothing is perturbed. \textit{Explanation drift} or \textit{decoupling}: explanations change while predictions stay the same. \textit{Collinearity}: strong correlation between features. \textit{Manipulation}: deliberate attacks that change explanations.}

\section{Review framework and information sources}\label{sec:sources}

Five databases were searched (Table~\ref{tab:sources}): IEEE Xplore and DBLP cover computer-science journals and conferences; ScienceDirect covers Elsevier journals, including operational-research and business titles; arXiv captures fast-moving machine-learning preprints; Semantic Scholar provides multidisciplinary coverage. \section{Data extraction and quality appraisal}
For each included study we extracted the Study ID, full citation, publication details and publication/review status with its metadata source, problem, RQs informed, method, datasets, evaluation, findings, limitations, reproducibility, quality scores with notes, and decision evidence. Metadata sources included Crossref, the arXiv record, the paper and Semantic Scholar; bibliographic registration alone was not treated as proof of peer review. Items a paper does not report are marked ``Not reported'' (Appendix~C; workbook sheets \texttt{Extraction}, \texttt{Quality} and \texttt{Quality\_Evidence}). Quality was appraised with ten items scored Yes = 1, Partial = 0.5, No = 0 or N/A: Q1 clear aims; Q2 replicable method; Q3 datasets described; Q4 appropriate comparison; Q5 well-defined stability measure; Q6 evidence sufficient (scale, repetitions, statistics); Q7 limitations discussed; Q8 code or data available; Q9 ethics, privacy or regulation addressed where relevant; Q10 randomness controlled and reported. The score is the share of applicable items; we rate 80\% or above as high, 60--79\% as moderate and below 60\% as low. Scores weight the synthesis but did not exclude any study; the Quality sheet summarizes concerns, and the supplied item-evidence table links every score to extracted evidence and its source locator. Appraisal was AI-assisted and source-linked; five ratings were corrected after dataset, code, seed or repetition evidence was checked. No independent duplicate quality re-extraction was performed. Quality scores are judgments, not a validation of the studies' claims. The mean score was \qaMean\%: \qaHigh{} studies were high, \qaMod{} moderate and \qaLow{} low \cite{S15,S55}. The weakest items were code or data availability (Q8, mean \qaQEight), ethics where applicable (Q9, \qaQNine{} over \qaQNineN{} applicable studies) and limitations (Q7, \qaQSeven). The included set meets the recency targets: \incSinceTwentyTwo{} of \totalIncluded{} studies are from 2022 or later and \incSinceTwentyFive{} from 2025 or later. It also contains \incBeforeTwentyTwo{} studies from before 2022, among them foundational work on explainer stability \cite{S01,S04,S08}, and \mapreview{} reviews \evreview. By type, there are \incJournal{} journal articles, \incConference{} conference papers, \incWorkshop{} workshop papers and \incPreprint{} preprints; the preprints are labelled as not peer reviewed. The set exceeds the minimum target because the topic is very active and RQ3 added a supplementary search. By question, \rqOne{} studies inform RQ1, \rqTwo{} RQ2 and \rqThree{} RQ3.

\section{Thematic synthesis and research gap}\label{sec:synth}
% SYNTHESIS-START
\textbf{Theme 1: stability depends on what is varied (RQ1).} Studies measure top-$k$ overlap, attribution dispersion, rank agreement or local sensitivity, but these endpoints are not interchangeable. S-LIME uses repeated-call Jaccard overlap \cite{S08}; VSI/CSI distinguish selected-variable agreement from coefficient agreement \cite{S10}; and local Lipschitz estimates compare nearby-input explanations \cite{S01,S05}. Other studies compare model refits or data conditions rather than repeated calls to one explainer. Hierarchical shrinkage measures SHAP variance across resampled training sets \cite{S83}, while ecological-risk analysis gives bootstrap attribution intervals \cite{S81}. These protocols answer different questions and should be recorded separately before results are compared. Attribution uncertainty is also distinct from uncertainty in the prediction itself.

\textbf{Theme 2: predictive performance is not a sufficient reliability check (RQ2).} The evidence concerns different prediction quantities and does not support one universal weak or strong relationship. In intrusion detection, the most accurate models did not necessarily give the most reproducible SHAP profiles \cite{S51}. Under the attacks evaluated by Rajhans and Khawarey, prediction robustness and attribution stability could diverge, although their estimates used separate held-out subsets \cite{S59}. Prediction-equivalent models from different hypothesis classes can also produce different attributions \cite{S75}; that is cross-model disagreement, not a local input-perturbation test. The newly included DDoS study relates fold-level SHAP consistency to model robustness, but conflicting correlation statements limit its numerical evidence \cite{S84}. Accuracy, calibration, prediction robustness and explanation stability therefore require separate operational definitions.

\textbf{Theme 3: sampling, model fitting and target choices contribute differently (RQ3).} LIME's random neighbourhood and hyperparameters affect repeated-call variation \cite{S08,S10}; control variates reduce KernelSHAP sampling variance, with gains varying by setting \cite{S18}. Collinearity can make global feature rankings change across refits: one preprint reports 52 of 77 benchmark datasets above a 10\% ranking-flip threshold, rather than demonstrating that every explanation is unstable \cite{S43}. Tree regularisation can reduce resampling variance in SHAP even when predictive gains are small \cite{S83}. Label manipulation changes smart-grid attributions \cite{S80}, while a bioprocess study retains 70\% of its top-ten SHAP features after changing the outcome threshold \cite{S82}. Neither target alteration is a prediction-preserving perturbation of one fixed instance. These distinctions prevent superficially similar stability results from being pooled as one outcome.

\textbf{Theme 4: warning scores have different targets and costs (RQ3).} Bayesian local explanations and bootstrap procedures attach intervals to attributions \cite{S05,S81}. Such uncertainty estimates may require additional samples or model fits. A single-model split-frequency screen flags feature pairs prone to global ranking reversals after retraining \cite{S43}; it is not a demonstrated per-instance detector of input-perturbation instability. The reliability score in S75 requires a reference ensemble and its explanations, so it is not computed from tree structure alone \cite{S75}. Forecast-uncertainty gating is associated with lower out-of-sample instability in synthetic experiments, but in-sample ordering can reverse and calibration needs held-out residuals \cite{S77}. Perturbation-free query evaluation therefore does not imply a cost-free or model-alone diagnostic.

\textbf{Evidence strength and gap.} Protocol heterogeneity, \incPreprint{} preprints, incomplete retrieval and individual reporting weaknesses limit generalisation. The current extraction identifies no study jointly testing instance-level prediction-to-attribution changes for both SHAP and LIME across logistic regression, random forest and XGBoost against each explainer's repeated-call noise floor. Predicted-class-preserving filters already exist \cite{S09,S25,S62}; their existence narrows the proposed contribution. Existing retraining diagnostics \cite{S43} also rule out claiming that tree-based warnings are new in general. The Assignment 1 problem should instead test the prediction--explanation link under explicit prediction-preserving perturbations, record split-threshold or bin-edge crossings, and evaluate whether an instance-level tree-derived score discriminates the observed instability. This is a gap in the retrieved, extracted evidence, not proof that no relevant research exists.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:lim}
(1) ACM Digital Library, Scopus and Web of Science were not searched; access limitations and an ACM bot check restricted coverage. OpenAlex was used only during piloting. (2) Phrase-based searches missed five of the \nKnown{} benchmark papers \cite{hwang2025,decker2025,lin2023,takefuji2025,hasnain2026}; they were not silently added outside the logged process. (3) \totalNotRetrieved{} of \totalSought{} sought reports could not be assessed from complete text, despite legal publisher/repository searches, creating possible retrieval bias. (4) Independent screening was performed on a 197-record sample, not the entire pool; extraction and quality appraisal were AI-assisted and not independently duplicated. Targeted source checks do not establish full reproducibility of every study. (5) Citation searching used an automated title filter; the supplementary RQ3 search omitted ScienceDirect. (6) \incPreprint{} included reports are preprints; version changes, weak reporting and heterogeneous stability targets limit certainty. The review is a thematic evidence synthesis, not a meta-analysis, and its gaps are bounded by the documented search and extraction.
