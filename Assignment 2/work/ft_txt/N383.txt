DEM Resolution Drives AI V ariability: Tradeoffs
Between Performance and Explainability in
Landslide Susceptibility Prediction
Yi Xia ,H a o y iY a o , Chao Ma, Feixiang Chen , and Y uanyuan Liu
Abstract—Artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) has shown strong predic-
tive capability in landslide susceptibility prediction (LSP), but its
closed-box nature creates a trust gap in geohazard decision support,
where predictive accuracy alone is insufﬁcient. High-resolution
digital elevation models (DEMs) are commonly expected to im-
prove terrain representation and prediction, yet it remains unclear
whether ﬁner DEMs also improve explanation reliability. This
study investigates the resolution-dependent mismatch between pre-
dictive performance and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)-
based explanation reliability in AI-based LSP . A 1 m Gaofen-7
DEM was used as the reference and resampled to 5, 10, 12.5, 30,
and 90 m. To isolate DEM-resolution effects, the slope-unit layer,
landslide labels, and DEM-derived input-factor types were kept
consistent across resolutions. Logistic regression (LR), support
vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), and XGBoost were
evaluated under repeated random seeds. A quantitative SHAP-
based framework integrating faithfulness, stability, and geomor-
phic consistency was further used to assess explanation reliabil-
ity. The results show that ﬁner DEMs, especially the 1 m DEM,
generally improved classiﬁcation performance and mapping efﬁ-
ciency. However, this predictive gain did not lead to a synchronous
improvement in explanation reliability. Faithfulness was largely
insensitive to DEM resolution, whereas stability and geomorphic
consistency showed resolution- and model-dependent responses.
Coarser DEMs could reduce attribution sensitivity through terrain
smoothing, but this apparent stability could be accompanied by
shifts in feature-importance structure, especially at 90 m and for
LR/SVM, where attribution moved from slope-related information

I. I NTRODUCTION
L
ANDSLIDES are among the most common and most
destructive geological hazards in mountainous areas [1],
[2], [3]. They have long posed signiﬁcant threats to human safety,
infrastructure, and regional economies [4], [5], [6]. Therefore,
landslide susceptibility prediction (LSP) has become a key
component of geological hazard prevention and control, as
well as territorial spatial planning [7], [8]. Although traditional
physical models have high physical consistency, they often rely
on detailed geotechnical parameters [9], [10]. This reliance
leads to excessive computational demands for regional-scale
mapping [11]. With advances in geospatial data acquisition and
computing power, artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) methods have been
widely applied to LSP [7], [8], [12], [13], [14]. These methods
have shown strong capability in capturing the complex nonlinear
relationships between landslide conditioning factors (LCFs) and
landslide occurrence [15], [16].
However, the rapid adoption of AI has created a critical
trust gap in geohazard management [17], [18], [19].M a n y
high-performance models remain opaque to practitioners, and
their decision logic is not transparent [12]. In the high-risk ﬁeld
of disaster risk reduction, predictive performance alone is not
sufﬁcient [20]. Models must also demonstrate explanation relia-
bility [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]. This means that the predictions
should be consistent with established geomorphological princi-
ples [26]. Although explainable AI frameworks, such as SHapley
Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [27], have begun to improve
model transparency by quantifying feature contributions [28],
the reliability of these explanations is highly sensitive to the
quality and size of the input data [29], [30], [31].
Among LSP input data, the digital elevation model (DEM)
plays a fundamental role [20], [32], [33]. In geoscience, a
common view is that higher spatial resolution can capture

ﬁner terrain features and therefore should produce better re-
sults [34], [35]. However, increasing evidence has revealed
a spatial resolution paradox: extremely ﬁne resolutions may
introduce microtopographic noise, such as single road features
or vegetation artifacts [36], [37]. This noise can obscure the
large-scale physical processes that drive slope instability [38].
These studies generally indicate that DEM resolution affects
LSP results. However, most existing studies focus on predictive
performance metrics, such as Accuracy and AUC. Research
on how DEM resolution affects model explainability remains
limited. This assessment is necessary because a DEM resolution
selected for high predictive accuracy may not provide equally
stable or geomorphologically consistent explanations. Such dif-
ferences matter when model explanations are used to support
susceptibility-based decisions.
This gap should be considered within the broader scale-
sensitivity literature in LSP . Earlier studies examined how raster
resolution inﬂuences conditioning-factor representation, factor
selection, and predictive performance. Arnone et al. [39] evalu-
ated raster resolution together with inventory rasterization and
found that the ﬁnest resolution did not necessarily yield the
best predictive performance. Liao et al. [40] further examined
resolution-dependent factor selection and its implications for
LSP . More recent work has extended scale analysis to model
interpretation. Hu et al. [41] combined multiscale slope-unit
delineation with XGBoost (XGB) and SHAP to examine factor
contributions and interactions. Related research has examined
how slope-unit scale affects factor selection and XAI-based
interpretation [42]. Pradhan et al. [43] used XGBoost and SHAP
to compare susceptibility predictions and feature contributions
between slope units and hydrological response units. Huang
et al. [44] compared grid- and slope-unit models using feature
importance, response curves, interaction analysis, and local
SHAP explanations. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that
spatial representation affects both predictive performance and
model interpretation. However, DEM resolution and mapping-
unit conﬁguration represent distinct sources of spatial variation.
DEM resolution controls the terrain information represented
within each slope unit, whereas slope-unit scale deﬁnes the
spatial support of modeling samples. Their respective effects
therefore need to be distinguished when assessing explanation
reliability. Speciﬁcally, it remains unclear whether ﬁner DEMs
improve explanation reliability when slope-unit boundaries are
held ﬁxed. Addressing this question requires evaluating predic-
tive performance alongside complementary aspects of explana-
tion reliability, including faithfulness, stability, and geomorphic
consistency.
To address this gap, this study investigates the joint effects
of DEM resolution on predictive performance and SHAP-based
explanation reliability in AI-based LSP . A 1 m Gaofen-7 (GF-7)
DEM was resampled to 5, 10, 12.5, 30, and 90 m. All DEMs
were derived from the same source, and slope-unit boundaries,
landslide labels, and DEM-derived input-factor types were kept
consistent across resolutions. This controlled design reduces
confounding from data-source heterogeneity and mapping-unit
changes, allowing DEM resolution to be treated as the primary
source of terrain-scale variation. Four representative models,
including LR, SVM, RF, and XGBoost, were evaluated under
repeated random seeds.
Compared with previous LSP studies, the main novelty of this
study lies in linking DEM resolution with explanation reliability
under a controlled experimental design. Speciﬁcally, this study
makes three contributions. First, it isolates DEM-resolution
effects from mapping-unit effects by deriving all DEMs from
the same GF-7 source and ﬁxing the slope-unit conﬁguration.
Second, it evaluates SHAP explanation reliability using faithful-
ness, stability, and geomorphic consistency, rather than relying
only on global feature ranking. These three criteria assess ﬁdelity
to model outputs, sensitivity to input perturbations, and agree-
ment with empirical geomorphic response patterns, respectively.
Together, they provide complementary diagnostics for exam-
ining explanation reliability across DEM resolutions. Third, it
shows that ﬁner DEMs improve predictive performance but do
not necessarily improve explanation reliability. DEM-resolution
selection should therefore be treated as an objective-dependent
tradeoff rather than being guided by the assumption that ﬁner
resolution is always preferable.
II. M A TERIALS
A. Study Area
The study area is located in the Huairou and Miyun districts of
northeastern Beijing, China, roughly between 40 ◦13’–41◦04’N
and 116◦16’–117◦30’E (see Fig. 1). The main exposed litholo-
gies consist of Archean metamorphic rocks (gneiss), Proterozoic
carbonate rocks (dolomite), and Mesozoic Jurassic volcanic-
sedimentary rocks (andesite and tuff). The Miyun Shachang–
Qiangzilu fault zone and the Huairou Changshaoying-Miyun
Gubeikou fault zone are the primary regional faults. The study
area lies within the Y anshan Mountain belt, characterized by
rugged terrain with an overall northwest-to-southeast slope gra-
dient. Elevations range from 40 m in the foreland plains to over
1700 m in the high mountain zones. Slopes are predominantly
covered by thin layers of mountain brown soils and leached
brown soils, averaging 20–50 cm in thickness. The study area
exhibits a warm-temperate, semihumid continental monsoon
climate, with an annual average temperature of approximately
11.6◦C and annual precipitation of 600–700 mm. Precipitation
distribution is highly uneven, with over 80% of annual rainfall
concentrated during the monsoon season from June to Septem-
ber. This often manifests as short-duration, high-intensity torren-
tial rain events, which are the primary trigger for local geological
hazards.
Over the past decade, the study area has long been a frequent
and severely impacted zone for landslides in Beijing [45], [46].
On 20 July 2016, torrential rains in North China triggered
multiple small-scale collapses and landslides in the northern
mountainous areas of Huairou District and around Miyun Reser-
voir, causing temporary trafﬁc disruptions. On 16 July 2018,
heavy rains in the Y unmeng Mountain area triggered landslides,
unleashing large-scale, clustered debris ﬂows that damaged sev-
eral villages and numerous transportation facilities. In late July
2023, Typhoon Doksuri triggered not only shallow landslides
but also numerous deep rock-mass landslides and high-elevation

B. Landslide Inventory
Using high-resolution PLANET remote sensing imagery ac-
quired before and after the “7 ·26” disaster, we identiﬁed 2998
event-related rainfall-induced shallow landslides through man-
ual visual interpretation, ﬁeld surveys, and supplementary UA V
imagery. The mapped landslides were mainly concentrated
around Liulimiao Town in Huairou District.
The inventory should be interpreted as an event-based inven-
tory of visually detectable rainfall-induced shallow landslides.
No a priori numerical minimum mapping size was imposed,
because practical detectability depends not only on image spa-
tial resolution, but also on surface exposure, vegetation cover,
shadow conditions, landslide morphology, and the contrast be-
tween pre and postevent images. Therefore, very small or weakly
exposed failures may be underrepresented.
Several sources of inventory uncertainty remain, including
image acquisition timing, vegetation and shadow occlusion,
ambiguity between source and runout areas, possible confusion
with erosion-related disturbed surfaces, and positional mismatch
among PLANET imagery, DEMs, and slope-unit boundaries.
These uncertainties may affect the absolute values of model per-
formance and local SHAP-based explanation patterns. However,
because the same inventory, slope-unit partitioning, and labeling
protocol were used for all DEM-resolution experiments, their
inﬂuence should be limited mainly to the common background
uncertainty rather than the relative comparison among DEM
resolutions.

and quantify the inﬂuence of DEM resolution on AI model
behavior. Including multisource factors with heterogeneous spa-

resolution, making it difﬁcult to attribute model performance and
explainability to DEM-derived information alone. Conversely,
DEM derivatives are directly and exclusively controlled by
DEM resolution, making them ideal indicators for assessing how

III. M ETHODS
This section outlines the workﬂow used to compare the im-
pacts of DEM resolution on AI performance and explainability
(see Fig. 2). First, data processing includes DEM resampling
and slope-unit delineation. Second, model performance metrics
are deﬁned and compared across AI models. Third, model ex-
plainability metrics are deﬁned and compared across AI models.
A. Data Preprocessing
1) DEM Resampling: A high-resolution DEM with a spatial
resolution of 1 m derived from GF-7 stereo imagery was used as
the reference dataset in our study. To systematically investigate
the effect of spatial resolution on LSP , the original 1 m DEM was
resampled to a series of coarser and representative resolutions,
including 5, 10, 12.5, 30, and 90 m [39], [51].A l lD E M s
were generated from the same source to ensure that differences
in model performance and explainability could be attributed

solely to changes in spatial resolution rather than data source
variability.
DEM resampling was performed using a consistent bilinear
interpolation method, ensuring that all derived DEMs differed
solely in spatial resolution while maintaining elevation continu-
ity and overall terrain characteristics [52], [53].
To examine whether the use of bilinear interpolation in-
troduced method-dependent artifacts, we further conducted a
resampling-method sensitivity analysis in Appendix F.I nt h i s
analysis, the original 1 m GF-7 DEM was resampled using
bilinear interpolation, nearest neighbor resampling, and cubic
convolution. DEM-derived factors were recalculated from each
resampled DEM and aggregated to the same slope-unit layer.
Pairwise Spearman distances of slope-unit mean factor values
and RF-based performance–explainability metrics were then
compared across the three resampling methods.
2) Slope Unit Delineation: Slope units were delineated using
the r.slopeunits tool and the related parameter-free slope-unit
terrain-subdivision framework [54], [55]. To keep the mapping-
unit framework consistent across all experiments, slope-unit
delineation was performed using the 30 m DEM resampled from
the original GF-7 dataset.
The 30 m DEM was selected because it represents an in-
termediate scale within the 1–90 m resolution series and pro-
vides a practical balance between preserving drainage-divide
terrain structure and avoiding excessive fragmentation [38].I n
this study, the ﬁxed 30 m slope-unit conﬁguration was used
as an experimental control rather than being interpreted as a
universally optimal slope-unit scale. Although slope-unit scale
can inﬂuence LSP [56], its systematic optimization is beyond
the main scope of this study. The same slope-unit layer was
therefore applied to all DEM resolutions. To examine whether
this choice affected the main conclusions, an additional slope-
unit sensitivity experiment using 10, 30, and 90 m slope-unit
conﬁgurations was conducted and reported in Appendix E.
3) Slope Unit Factor Integration: Slope units were used as
the minimum spatial units for LSP . They represent terrain-based
geomorphological units bounded by drainage and divide lines,
and are more consistent with hillslope processes than indi-
vidual raster pixels. This choice is consistent with previous
studies showing that slope-unit-based frameworks can provide
geomorphologically meaningful alternatives to pixel-based sus-
ceptibility or hazard mapping, especially for ﬁrst-time failures
or heterogeneous landslide inventories [57], [58]. Therefore,
the modeling workﬂow was slope-unit-based rather than pixel-
based.
The ten DEM-derived raster factors were overlaid with the
ﬁxed slope-unit polygons and summarized using zonal statistics
in ArcGIS Pro. For each slope unit, the mean value of each factor
was assigned to the slope-unit attribute table, so that each unit
formed one modeling sample with a consistent terrain-attribute
vector.
Landslide labels were generated by spatially joining the in-
ventory with the slope-unit layer. Units containing at least one
mapped landslide were labeled as landslide-present, whereas
units without mapped landslides were treated as candidate non-
landslide units for subsequent sampling. These candidate units
represent pseudoabsence rather than conﬁrmed stable samples,
because unmapped or very small failures may still be present.

C. XAI Methods
1) SHapley Additive Explanations: To evaluate explanation
reliability under different DEM resolutions, the SHAP frame-
work was employed. SHAP quantiﬁes the contribution of each
input feature to an individual model prediction by computing
Shapley values derived from cooperative game theory, ensuring
local accuracy and consistency of feature attributions [27].
In our study, SHAP values were computed using model-
speciﬁc explainers according to the underlying learning algo-
rithm. For LR, a linear SHAP explainer was adopted. For SVM,
which does not have a model-speciﬁc SHAP formulation under
nonlinear kernel settings, a model-agnostic SHAP explainer was
adopted. For tree-based models (RF and XGB), a TreeExplainer
implemented with GPU acceleration was used to efﬁciently
estimate SHAP values.
For each random seed, the corresponding training subset was
used as the SHAP background dataset, and SHAP values were
computed for all testing samples. The featurewise mean of the
training subset was used as the masking baseline in faithful-
ness and related perturbation-based evaluations. The complete
SHAP explainer settings and masking parameters are reported
in Appendix Table IX.
2) Explainability Metrics: To quantitatively assess model
explainability, a set of SHAP-based evaluation metrics was
employed. These metrics are independent of predictive perfor-
mance and include faithfulness, stability, and consistency.
Explanation quality is multifaceted and cannot be fully char-
acterized by a single metric. Therefore, we treat the pro-
posed metrics as complementary operational proxies rather
than exhaustive deﬁnitions of explanation quality. Faithful-
ness is aligned with perturbation/deletion-based ﬁdelity and
inﬁdelity evaluation. Stability is aligned with perturbation-
sensitivity evaluation. Geomorphic consistency is designed as an
application-grounded domain-prior alignment metric for LSP .
These metrics were developed based on established XAI eval-
uation protocols [66], [67]. To further verify this, we conducted a
deletion-based perturbation test, a masking-inﬁdelity alignment
test, and a randomized-label sanity check for benchmark testing
(see Appendix D). The proposed metrics should be interpreted as
complementary operational proxies for explanation credibility
rather than exhaustive deﬁnitions of explanation quality.

portant by SHAP are actually inﬂuential for the model output. It
follows the logic of perturbation/deletion-based XAI evaluation,

ing model-output change is measured[68], [69], [70]. Therefore,
faithfulness should be interpreted as model-behavior ﬁdelity
rather than as a sufﬁcient condition for geomorphic correctness.
It was evaluated by examining the relationship between fea-
ture attributions and the corresponding changes in model output.

D. Experimental Settings
All modeling samples were deﬁned at the slope-unit level.
Slope units containing at least one mapped landslide were re-
tained as landslide-present samples, whereas slope units without
mapped landslides were regarded as candidate nonlandslide
samples. For each random seed, nonlandslide samples were
randomly selected without replacement from the candidate pool
to match the number of landslide-present samples, producing
a balanced binary dataset with a 1:1 class ratio. No additional
spatial declustering was applied in the main experiments; inven-
tory clustering was treated as an invariant background condition
because the same inventory and sampling protocol were used
across all DEM-resolution experiments.
For each seed, the balanced dataset was divided into
training, validation, and testing subsets using a stratiﬁed
60%/20%/20% split. Stratiﬁcation preserved the 1:1 class ratio
in each subset. The same seed-speciﬁc sample selection and
train/validation/test split were shared by all four models and all
DEM-resolution experiments, so that differences among models
and DEM resolutions were not introduced by inconsistent sam-
pling or data partitioning. The testing subset was used only for
reporting predictive performance and SHAP-based explainabil-
ity metrics.
All experiments were repeated using ten random seeds to
reduce dependence on a single nonlandslide selection or data
partition. The reported metrics are the mean values across the
ten repetitions, and the corresponding standard deviations de-
scribe variability associated with nonlandslide sampling, dataset
partitioning, and model training stochasticity.

V. D ISCUSSION
AI has been rapidly integrated into geohazard modeling [8],
[13], [14]. This process has often been guided by the common
heuristic that higher spatial resolution necessarily leads to better
predictive performance [34], [35]. Our results do not simply
reject the predictive value of ﬁne-resolution terrain data; rather,
they show that this heuristic is incomplete when explanation reli-
ability is also considered. Under the controlled DEM-resolution
experiment, ﬁner DEMs generally improved predictive perfor-
mance and zoning efﬁciency, but the same resolutions did not
consistently produce more stable or geomorphologically con-
sistent SHAP explanations. Conversely, coarser DEMs could
reduce attribution sensitivity through spatial smoothing, but
this apparent stability may be accompanied by shifts in the
geomorphic information used by the models. Therefore, the
central implication is that DEM resolution should be treated as an
objective-dependent tradeoff: prediction-oriented applications
may beneﬁt from ﬁne-resolution DEMs, whereas applications
requiring defensible explanations should jointly evaluate pre-
dictive performance and explanation reliability.
A. Spatial Resolution Paradox: Performance V ersus
Explainability
The results reveal a clear divergence between predictive per-
formance and SHAP-based explanation reliability. Fine DEMs,
especially the 1 m DEM, generally produce the strongest clas-
siﬁcation performance and the sharpest delineation of high-
susceptibility zones. However, this predictive advantage does
not translate into a synchronous improvement in all explanation
indicators. Faithfulness remains almost invariant across DEM
resolutions, indicating that this metric is not the main source
of the observed performance–explanation mismatch under the
present masking baseline. The resolution-sensitive part of ex-
planation reliability is instead expressed mainly through attri-
bution stability, geomorphic consistency, and resolution-driven
feature-importance drift.
At ﬁne resolution, DEM-derived factors preserve local terrain
conditions associated with rainfall-induced shallow landslides,
such as steep hillslope gradients and small-scale terrain vari-
ation. These ﬁne-scale signals can improve class separability
and mapping efﬁciency. At the same time, ultra-ﬁne DEMs may
also retain high-frequency components caused by small surface
irregularities, vegetation effects, human modiﬁcations, or local
DEM artifacts (see Fig. 11) [37], [78], [79]. These components
can increase the sensitivity of SHAP attribution vectors to small
input perturbations. Therefore, the reduced stability observed
at ﬁne resolutions should be interpreted as increased attribution

At coarser resolutions, spatial aggregation acts as a low-pass
ﬁlter by smoothing terrain variability and reducing local per-
turbability [80]. This helps explain why attribution stability often
improves toward the coarse end of the resolution sequence,
although the most stable resolution is model-dependent. LR,
SVM, and XGB show their strongest or progressively improved
stability at 90 m, whereas RF reaches its strongest stability
at 30 m. This pattern indicates that coarse DEMs can reduce
attribution sensitivity, but such stability should not be interpreted
as direct evidence of more reliable geomorphological reasoning.
Geomorphic consistency provides a complementary perspec-
tive. Unlike stability, which evaluates perturbation sensitivity,
geomorphic consistency evaluates whether attribution directions
agree with empirical geomorphic priors. The results show that
consistency is also resolution- and model-dependent: RF reaches
its highest consistency at 5 m, XGB at 30 m, whereas LR and
SVM reach their highest consistency at 90 m. This conﬁrms
that explanation reliability cannot be represented by stability
alone. Moreover, high consistency at coarse resolution should
still be interpreted together with feature-importance structure.
At 90 m, LR and SVM show a marked attribution shift from
local slope-related information toward TRI, suggesting that the
model explanation may become more stable while relying on a
different, broader terrain descriptor.
This is the spatial-resolution paradox observed in this
study. Fine DEMs improve predictive performance by pre-
serving discriminative terrain detail, but they can increase
attribution sensitivity. Coarse DEMs can stabilize SHAP attribu-
tions through terrain smoothing, but this stability may coincide
with simpliﬁed or shifted geomorphic information. Faithfulness
remains largely insensitive to DEM resolution, so it does not
resolve this tradeoff. Therefore, explanation reliability should
be interpreted jointly through faithfulness, stability, geomorphic
consistency, and feature-importance drift rather than through any
single metric. The geomorphological meaning of the observed
attribution drift is examined in the next section in relation to
rainfall-induced shallow-slope failure mechanics.
It should be emphasized that the mechanisms discussed
here are interpretations of empirical patterns rather than direct
causal proof. The experiments show that DEM coarsening is
associated with lower predictive performance, lower attribu-
tion sensitivity in several cases, and resolution-dependent shifts
in SHAP feature importance. The low-pass ﬁltering effect,
microtopographic-noise explanation, and different responses of
linear, kernel-based, and tree-ensemble models provide plausi-
ble interpretations of these patterns, but they do not demonstrate
a complete physical causal pathway. Therefore, the discussion
distinguishes between observed empirical regularities and their
geomorphological or algorithmic interpretation.
B. Geomorphological Grounding of Resolution-Driven
Attribution Drift
The resolution-driven changes in SHAP feature importance
should be interpreted in relation to the dominant slope-process
mechanics of rainfall-induced shallow landslides. In the present
study area, the mapped failures are mainly shallow landslides
triggered by short-duration, high-intensity rainfall under thin
soil-cover conditions. Under such conditions, local slope gra-
dient is expected to be a ﬁrst-order terrain control because it is
directly related to gravitational driving stress, runoff concentra-
tion, and shallow slope-failure thresholds [20], [26], [77].H y -
drological and local-shape variables, such as TWI and curvature-
related factors, may further reﬂect local water accumulation and
hillslope morphology. By contrast, RDL, TRI, CV , elevation,
and aspect are better interpreted as broader terrain-context or
roughness descriptors rather than direct substitutes for local
slope-failure mechanisms [81].

From this perspective, the attribution structures observed
at ﬁne-to-intermediate resolutions are more consistent with
shallow-slope failure mechanics. As shown in Figs. 8 and 9,
LR and SVM are mainly dominated by slope from 1 to 30 m,
with RDL and TRI acting as secondary relief or roughness
descriptors. This suggests that, when DEM resolution is ﬁne
enough to preserve hillslope-scale gradients, the models still rely
primarily on local slope-process information. For RF and XGB,
feature importance is more distributed among elevation, slope,
CV , RDL, and TRI, indicating that tree-ensemble models may
capture nonlinear combinations between local gradient, terrain
dissection, and broader geomorphic context.
The attribution pattern at 90 m requires more caution. For
LR and SVM, the dominant feature shifts from slope to TRI,
while the contribution of slope decreases sharply (see Fig. 9).
This shift should not be interpreted as evidence that macroscale
roughness is physically more important than local slope for
shallow landslide initiation. One plausible interpretation is that
DEM coarsening may smooth local gradient information and
lead simpler models to rely more strongly on broader roughness
proxies. Therefore, the higher attribution stability observed at
coarse resolution does not necessarily imply stronger geomor-
phological credibility. Instead, it may reﬂect a scale-induced
simpliﬁcation of terrain representation. Taken together, these
observations suggest that attribution drift is not only a statistical
pattern, but may also reﬂect changes in the type of geomorphic
information used by the models under the tested setting.
C. Why Does a Coarser DEM Reduce Performance but
Sometimes Improve Explainability?
The following interpretation is based on comparative empiri-
cal patterns rather than direct causal evidence of terrain-process
mechanisms. From a terrain-information perspective, DEM res-
olution can be interpreted as inﬂuencing the frequency-band cov-
erage of geomorphic signals[82]. Fine resolution preserves more
high-frequency microtopographic details. Coarse resolution can
be conceptually understood as applying a stronger low-pass
ﬁltering effect to the terrain surface. For LSP , this change may
propagate through DEM-derived factors. It can affect slope, cur-
vature, wetness indices, roughness, and related variables. Their
statistical distributions, local gradients, and second-derivative
structures all show resolution-dependent variations (see Fig.12).
The overall decline in performance at coarser resolutions sug-
gests that, in our study area, ﬁne-scale terrain variability still pro-
vides information gain for separating unstable and stable slope
units under rainfall-induced shallow landslide mechanisms. This
aligns with the intuition that higher resolution can help. At
the same time, our results show model-dependent empirical
differences. Tree-ensemble models, including RF and XGB,
degrade more slowly as resolution becomes coarser. Linear
and kernel-based methods, including LR and SVM, are more
sensitive. This suggests that signal loss at coarser resolution
is not only noise reduction. It also changes class separability
and feature interaction patterns. One possible explanation is
that tree models are better suited to representing geomorphic
processes with nonlinear thresholds, interactions, and piecewise
responses [64], [83]. This may help them retain more stable
discriminative ability under scale changes. In contrast, LR and
SVM may rely more on direct separating boundaries. They
may therefore be more affected by distribution shifts caused by
changes in scale, especially when kernel parameters and sample
splits vary across repeated trials [84].
In contrast to performance, SHAP stability is often better
at coarser resolution. This reﬂects a key but often overlooked
point. Stability depends on both the model and the perturbability
and local smoothness of the input signal [85], [86]. When the
DEM is coarser, local variability and high-frequency ﬂuctu-
ations in derived factors tend to be suppressed. Under input
perturbations of the same normalized magnitude, the response
in the model-output space is more likely to be smooth and
approximately linear. This may lead to smaller shifts in SHAP
attribution vectors. In other words, coarse resolution is not
necessarily more physically realistic. However, it may produce
more stable attributions because it can weaken the ampliﬁcation
of ﬁne-scale noise and local discontinuities in explanations[82],
[85], [87].
This provides an AI-based interpretation of the resolution
paradox in geoscience. Microtopographic details can reﬂect
real process signals, but they can also represent measurement
errors, object artifacts, or high-frequency structures that are
weakly related to landslide mechanisms. Models may not reli-
ably separate these components a priori during learning. There-
fore, under the tested setting, the beneﬁts of high-resolution
data can be accompanied by the cost of reduced explanation
stability.
The additional resampling-method sensitivity analysis in
Appendix F further suggests that interpolation artifacts may
affect local numerical values of curvature-derived factors, but
they did not substantially alter the RF-based performance, SHAP
stability, consistency, or dominant Top-3 SHAP factors under the
tested setting.
D. Inﬂuence of the Fixed Slope-Unit Framework
The ﬁxed 30 m slope-unit framework is an experimental
control rather than a claim that 30 m is the optimal mapping-unit
scale. This design helps isolate the effect of DEM resolution
by preventing the mapping units, landslide labels, and sample
deﬁnitions from changing simultaneously with raster resolution.
However, it may also partially anchor the analysis to an inter-
mediate terrain-partition scale. For very ﬁne DEMs, slope-unit
aggregation may suppress part of the pixel-level terrain vari-
ability; for very coarse DEMs, several DEM cells may represent
the internal terrain structure of one slope unit only coarsely.
Therefore, the reported resolution-dependent tradeoff should be
interpreted within this ﬁxed slope-unit modeling framework.
The additional slope-unit sensitivity analysis in Appendix E
indicates that the main performance–explanation mismatch re-
mains visible under representative 10, 30, and 90 m slope-unit
conﬁgurations. This suggests that the mismatch is not solely
caused by the 30 m slope-unit choice. Nevertheless, the exact
numerical values of performance and SHAP-based explainabil-
ity metrics may vary with slope-unit scale. Future studies should

E. Implications for DEM-Resolution Selection in Trustworthy
AI
These ﬁndings challenge the common “ﬁner is better” view in
geospatial AI. If the primary objective is predictive discrimina-
tion or emergency-oriented susceptibility mapping, then using a
ﬁne-resolution DEM can be advantageous because it preserves
terrain details that improve model separability. However, when
the objective also includes explanation reliability, geomorpho-
logical interpretability, or defensible decision support, DEM
resolution cannot be selected solely according to predictive
accuracy [21], [26].
The results show that ﬁner DEMs and stronger predictive
performance do not necessarily correspond to more reliable
explanations. Explanation metrics respond to DEM resolution
in a metric-speciﬁc manner, and stable attributions at coarser
resolutions may partly result from spatial smoothing rather
than more physically meaningful reasoning. Therefore, DEM
resolution should be treated as a modeling design parameter
rather than a universally optimized input setting. Researchers
should explicitly evaluate whether the selected DEM resolution
supports the intended balance between predictive performance
and explanation reliability.
F . Limitations and Transferability
Several limitations should be considered when transferring
the ﬁndings to other regions or modeling tasks. First, the land-
slide inventory used here is an event-based inventory of visually
detectable rainfall-induced shallow landslides. The conclusions
may differ for deep-seated landslides, rockfalls, debris ﬂows,
or multitemporal inventories. Second, the predictor set was
intentionally restricted to DEM-derived factors to isolate DEM-
resolution effects. In operational LSP , lithology, rainfall, vege-
tation, land use, faults, roads, and human activity may interact
with DEM resolution and alter both performance and SHAP
explanations. Third, nonlandslide samples were constructed as
pseudoabsence samples, and the spatial clustering of landslides
was treated as an invariant background condition rather than
fully declustered. These choices support controlled comparison
across DEM resolutions, but they limit direct claims about abso-
lute model generalization. Future work should test the proposed
framework using independent event inventories, spatial-block
validation, density-stratiﬁed sampling, and multisource condi-
tioning factors.
G. Broader Implications for Geospatial AI
The implications of this mismatch extend beyond landslide
susceptibility. Remote sensing is advancing rapidly, and sub-
meter global terrain data are becoming more common [88],
[89], [90]. Earth science will soon face a ﬂood of ultra-high-
resolution data. Our study offers an important warning. Feeding
high-ﬁdelity data into closed-box algorithms does not automat-
ically produce more reliable science. Trustworthy AI for Earth
systems requires more than single-dimensional performance
metrics. Future research frameworks should jointly optimize
predictive performance and explanation reliability. This will
ensure that AI not only gives the correct answer, but also does
so for the correct physical reasons.
VI. C ONCLUSION
Earth observation and terrain data are now widely available.
LSP often assumes that ﬁner resolution is always better. Our
study examined whether ﬁner DEM resolution necessarily im-
proves AI-based LSP from both predictive performance and
explanation reliability. To isolate DEM-resolution effects, all
DEMs were derived from the same 1 m GF-7 source, while
slope-unit conﬁguration, landslide labels, and DEM-derived
predictor types were kept consistent across experiments. We
further developed a SHAP-based evaluation framework inte-
grating faithfulness, stability, and geomorphic consistency, so
that explanation reliability can be evaluated quantitatively rather
than inferred only from feature-importance rankings. Additional
slope-unit and resampling-method sensitivity analyses indicate
that the main performance–explanation mismatch is not solely
an artifact of the ﬁxed 30 m slope-unit conﬁguration or bilinear
resampling.
Three main ﬁndings emerge as follows.
1) Predictive performance was generally stronger at ﬁner
DEM resolutions. Across LR, SVM, RF, and XGBoost
under repeated random seeds, the 1 m DEM was the
most consistently favorable resolution for classiﬁcation
performance and mapping efﬁciency, whereas the 90 m
DEM generally showed the weakest predictive perfor-
mance. This pattern was especially clear for tree-ensemble
models, although some metric-speciﬁc optima occurred at
5–10 m for individual models.
2) Explanation reliability did not improve synchronously
with predictive performance. Faithfulness was largely
insensitive to DEM resolution, whereas stability and
geomorphic consistency showed clear scale- and model-
dependent responses. This indicates that predictive met-
rics alone cannot determine whether a DEM resolution
provides reliable model explanations.
3) Coarser DEMs often reduced attribution sensitivity,
but more stable attributions did not necessarily imply
stronger geomorphological credibility. At 90 m, LR and
SVM showed a pronounced attribution shift from slope-
dominated patterns toward broader roughness proxies,
such as TRI, which may weaken the link between model
reasoning and rainfall-induced shallow-slope failure me-
chanics. By contrast, tree ensembles, particularly RF,
showed less abrupt attribution reranking and maintained
comparatively higher geomorphic consistency across
resolutions.
Together, these ﬁndings reveal a performance–explanation
mismatch that leads to an objective-dependent DEM-resolution
tradeoff in AI-based LSP . Finer DEMs can improve class sep-
arability and zoning efﬁciency by preserving discriminative
terrain detail, but this predictive gain does not necessarily lead
to more stable or geomorphologically consistent explanations.

Conversely, coarser DEMs can stabilize SHAP attributions
through terrain smoothing, but they may also simplify or shift
the geomorphic information used by the models. Therefore, no
single DEM resolution dominates all performance and explana-
tion criteria. DEM resolution should be treated as an explicit,
objective-dependent modeling design parameter rather than as
a one-directional “ﬁner is better” setting. For the present study
area and DEM-only predictor set, prediction-oriented applica-
tions may beneﬁt from ﬁne-resolution DEMs, whereas appli-
cations emphasizing defensible explanation should jointly ex-
amine predictive performance and explanation reliability before
selecting a DEM resolution.
These conclusions are bounded by the event-based landslide
inventory, the DEM-only predictor set, and the tested slope-
unit framework. Future work should extend this framework to
multisource predictors, including lithology, land use, rainfall,
vegetation, and human activities, and should test its transfer-
ability across different climatic regions, landslide types, and
mapping-unit schemes. Spatially uneven landslide distribution
and inventory-density clustering should also be examined ex-
plicitly using spatial declustering, density-stratiﬁed sampling,
spatial-block validation, or independent event inventories. These
steps are needed to support trustworthy geospatial AI for risk
reduction and spatial planning.

for each factor and each interval, we compute the Pearson

interval-dependent geomorphic responses rather than enforcing
a global linear assumption.
