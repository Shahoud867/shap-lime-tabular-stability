Abstract— Mortality prediction in the intensive care unit represents a high-stakes clinical task in which machine learning models 
have demonstrated substantial predictive advantages over conventional severity scoring systems. However, the opacity of high -
performing gradient boosting models impedes clinical adoption, undermines regulatory compliance, and limits the actionability 
of risk predictions. This paper presents a rigorous technical investigation of three post -hoc explanation methods —TreeSHAP, 
ensemble LIME, a nd Anchors —implemented and systematically compared on XGBoost and LightGBM mortality prediction 
models trained on the MIMIC-IV critical care database, encompassing 48,372 ICU admissions and 64 clinical features. A multi -
criteria evaluation framework assessing faithfulness, stability, and comprehensibility is developed and applied, with quantitative 
metrics including the Area Over the Perturbation Curve, Lipschitz continuity estimation, and explanation entropy. Experimenta l 
results demonstrate that TreeSHAP achieves superior faithfulness (AOPC: 0.276) and stability (Lipschitz estimate: 0.312) whilst 
generating explanations in 4 milliseconds per instance, supporting real -time clinical deployment. Anchors produce the most 
comprehensible explanations through compact if-then rules but exhibit lower faithfulness and limited patient coverage. Ensemble 
LIME occupies an intermediate position across all dimensions. These findings provide evidence -based guidance for explanation 
method selection in clinical AI deployment  contexts subject to the transparency obligations of the EU Artificial Intelligence Act 
and GDPR Article 22. 
 
Keywords— Clinical decision support, Explainable artificial intelligence, ICU mortality prediction, Machine learning, MIMIC -

1. Introduction  
 
The intensive care unit (ICU) represents one of the most 
clinically complex and resource -intensive environments in 
modern medicine, where timely and accurate mortality risk 
stratification is essential for informing treatment escalation, 
resource allocation , and goals -of-care decisions [1]. 
Conventional severity -of-illness scoring systems, including 
APACHE II [2], SOFA [3], and SAPS II [4], have long 
provided the clinical standard for ICU prognostication. 
However, these hand -crafted scores are constrained by  their 
reliance on a limited number of pre -selected variables, their 
assumption of linear predictor-outcome relationships, and their 
inability to exploit the full breadth of longitudinal data 
captured in contemporary electronic health record (EHR) 
systems [5]. 
 
The availability of large -scale critical care databases, most 
notably the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care 
version IV (MIMIC -IV) [6], has enabled the application of

machine learning (ML) methods that substantially outperform 
conventional severity scores in mortality prediction tasks. 
Gradient boosting algorithms and deep neural networks have 
achieved area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 
(AUROC) values consistently exceeding 0.85 across multiple 
prediction horizons on MIMIC -derived cohorts [7], [8]. 
Despite these predictive gains, the clinical translation of ML -
based prognostic systems has been significantly impeded by 
the fundamental opacity of high -performing models [9]. 
Clinicians who cannot understand the reasoning underlying a 
mortality risk prediction are rationally reluctant to act upon it, 
particularly when model outputs conflict with clinical intuition 
or when patients present atypically relati ve to training 
populations [10]. Furthermore, a mortality risk score without 
an accompanying explanation of its driving features offers 
limited actionable guidance for therapeutic intervention which 
is a critical deficiency in a setting where understanding  the 
modifiable determinants of predicted risk is of direct clinical 
consequence [11]. 
 
The imperative for explainability in clinical AI has been 
reinforced by an increasingly stringent regulatory landscape. 
The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation 
(GDPR) [12] confers rights upon individuals subject to 
automated decisions with significant effects, including a right 
to meaningful explanation. The EU Artificial Intelligence Act 
[13], adopted in 2024, classifies clinical decision support 
systems as high -risk AI applications subject to mandatory 
transparency and human oversight obli gations. In the United 
Kingdom, the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory 
Agency (MHRA) [14] and the National Institute for Health and 
Care Excellence (NICE) [15] have both emphasised 
explainability as a prerequisite for the safe deployment of AI -
based medical devices. These regulatory developments 
establish explainability not merely as a scientific desideratum 
but as a legal and governance requirement. 
 
The field of Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) has 
produced a diverse portfolio of post -hoc explanation methods 
designed to illuminate the predictive logic of opaque ML 
models [16]. Among the most widely adopted in the clinical 
ML literature are SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [17], 
Local Interpretable Model -Agnostic Explanations (LIME) 
[18], and Anchor explanations [19] —three methodologically 
distinct frameworks operationalising fundamentally different 
conceptions of meaningful explanation. SH AP grounds 
explanation in cooperative game theory, computing Shapley 
values that quantify each feature's marginal contribution to a 
prediction with guarantees of local accuracy, consistency, and 
missingness [20]. The TreeSHAP variant [21] enables exact 
and computationally efficient Shapley value computation for 
tree-based models, making SHAP particularly well -suited to 
tabular EHR data. LIME fits a locally weighted linear 
surrogate model to the neighbourhood of the instance being 
explained, generating featu re importance coefficients 
interpretable as linear approximations of the local decision 
boundary [18]. Anchors depart from additive attribution and 
linear approximation, instead generating if -then rule -based 
explanations identifying a sufficient set of fea ture conditions 
under which a prediction remains stable with high probability 
[19], a form particularly aligned with rule -based clinical 
reasoning. 
 
Despite the growing body of clinical ML literature, a 
systematic, head -to-head comparison of SHAP, LIME, and 
Anchors implemented on a large -scale, contemporary critical 
care dataset with rigorous multi-criteria quantitative evaluation 
remains notably absent [22]. Existing comparative studies have 
tended to focus on a single evaluation criterion applied to small 
or proprietary datasets that do not permit reproducible 
benchmarking [23]. Furthermore, prior work has rarely 
examined the clinical alignment of generated explanations, the 
degree to which explanation outputs correspond to 
physiological variables recognised by clinical experts as 
genuinely prognostic in critical illness , nor systematically 
investigated explanation stability across similar patient 
presentations, a property of considerable practical importance 
in time-pressured ICU environments [24]. 
 
The present paper addresses these gaps through  two specific 
contributions. First, it presents complete, reproducible 
implementations of SHAP ( TreeSHAP), LIME, and Anchors 
applied to XGBoost and LightGBM mortality prediction 
models trained on MIMIC -IV, encompassing over 500,000 
hospital admissions [6]. Second, it develops and applies a 
multi-criteria quantitative evaluation framework assessing 
explanation faithfulness (Area Over the Perturbation Curve, 
AOPC), stability (Lipschitz continuity estimation), and 
comprehensibility (explanation complexity metrics). 
 
2. Related Work  
 
2.1 Machine Learning for ICU Mortality Prediction 
The application of ML to ICU mortality prediction has 
advanced considerably over the past decade, with gradient 
boosting methods and deep learning architectures consistently 
outperforming conventional severity scores across diverse 
critical care population s. Rajkomar et al. [25] demonstrated 
that deep learning models trained on longitudinal EHR data 
achieved AUROC values of 0.93 for in -hospital mortality 
prediction across two large academic medical centres, 
substantially exceeding APACHE and SOFA baseline 
performance. Subsequent work by Moor et al. [26] established 
that temporal convolutional networks applied to irregularly 
sampled physiological time series achieved competitive 
performance on MIMIC -III benchmarks whilst exhibiting 
superior calibration relati ve to recurrent architectures —a 
property of considerable clinical importance given that well -
calibrated risk estimates are prerequisite for meaningful 
clinical interpretation. 
 
Gradient boosting frameworks have emerged as the dominant 
modelling paradigm for tabular EHR data, owing to their 
superior handling of missing values, robustness to feature 
scaling, and compatibility with post-hoc explanation methods. 
Conducted a systematic comparison of random forest, 
XGBoost, logistic regression, and artificial neural network 
models for ICU mortality prediction on the MIMIC -III 
database, finding that XGBoost achieved the highest AUROC 
(0.874) whilst exhibiting substant ially lower computational

evaluated LightGBM against deep learning baselines on

for clinical deployment cont exts where model maintenance 
resources are constrained [28]. 
 
A notable limitation across the ICU mortality prediction 
literature concerns the generalisation of models trained on 
single-centre datasets to external clinical environments. 
Demonstrated substantial performance degradation when 
MIMIC-III trained models were evaluated on data from a 
geographically distinct ICU, attributing the decline to 
distributional shift in both patient demographics and clinical 
coding practices [29]. This finding underscores the importance 
of developing explanation frameworks capable of identifying 
the specific feature distributions driving predictions, enabling 
clinicians to assess the plausibility of model outputs in light of 
local patient population characteristics. 
 
2.2 Post-Hoc Explainability Methods in Clinical ML 
SHAP in Healthcare Applications 
SHAP has achieved widespread adoption in clinical ML owing 
to its theoretical grounding in cooperative game theory and its 
provision of consistent, locally accurate feature attributions . 
Demonstrated the clinical utility of TreeSHAP in a landmark 
study identifying previously unrecognised interaction effects 
between blood oxygen saturation and respiratory rate in sepsis 
mortality prediction —a finding that subsequently informed 
clinical guide line revision  [20]. Applied SHAP to early 
warning score models trained on Danish hospital EHR data, 
demonstrating that SHAP -generated feature attributions 
aligned closely with clinician judgement regarding the most 
prognostically significant physiological parameters, with 
lactate, mean arterial pressure, and Glasgow Coma Scale 
identified as consistently dominant contributors across patient 
subgroups [30]. 
 
Notably, examined the faithfulness of SHAP explanations in 
clinical settings under conditions of feature correlation —a 
pervasive characteristic of physiological EHR data —finding 
that correlated features can produce misleading attribution 
distributions in which clin ical relevance is arbitrarily 
partitioned among collinear predictors  [31]. This finding has 
significant implications for ICU mortality prediction contexts, 
where variables such as lactate, base excess, and bicarbonate 
exhibit strong physiological correlations, and motivates the 
inclusion of SHAP interaction value analysis as a 
complementary explanation tool. 
 
LIME in Healthcare Applications 
LIME has been applied across a range of clinical prediction 
tasks, though its adoption in critical care settings has been 
tempered by concerns regarding explanation instability. 
Shankaranarayana and Runje [32] applied LIME to mortality 
prediction models trained on the eICU database, reporting that 
whilst LIME -generated explanations were rated as clinically 
plausible by intensivists in 68% of cases, repeated application 
of LIME to identical patient instances produced substantially 
different feature rankings in 31% of cases —a instability rate 
considered clinically unacceptable in time -sensitive decision 
contexts. Further demonstrated that LIME explanations are 
susceptible to adversarial manipulation, wherein a classifier 
can be constructed to exhibit discriminatory behaviour whilst 
producing innocuous LIME explanations —a finding with 
direct implications for the use of LIME in regulatory 
compliance contexts [33]. 
 
Anchors in Healthcare Applications 
Anchors have received comparatively limited evaluation in 
clinical ML contexts relative to SHAP and LIME, representing 
a gap that the present study directly addresses. Rabold et al. 
[34] applied Anchor explanations to a diabetes readmission 
prediction model, finding that clinicians rated anchor rules as 
significantly more comprehensible than SHAP waterfall plots 
(mean Likert score 4.1 vs. 3.2, p < 0.05), though with 
substantially lower explanation coverage (mean 0.31), 
indicating that anchor rules applied t o fewer than one-third of 
patient instances. This coverage limitation is particularly 
consequential in ICU settings characterised by high patient 
heterogeneity, where a low -coverage explanation system 
would leave the majority of clinical predictions unexplained. 
 
Comparative Evaluation of XAI Methods in Clinical 
Contexts 
Comparative evaluation of multiple XAI methods within a 
single clinical study remains relatively uncommon, with most 
published work evaluating a single explanation method in 
isolation. Conducted a systematic review of 62 XAI studies 
using real-world EHR data, finding that SHAP was the most 
frequently employed method (47% of studies), followed by 
LIME (23%), with rule -based methods including Anchors 
representing only 8% of studies  [23]. Crucially, only 11% of 
reviewed studies employed more than one explanation method, 
and fewer than 5% conducted formal comparative evaluation 
using quantitative explanation quality metrics. 
 
Among the comparative studies that do exist, [35] provided an 
important theoretical and empirical analysis of explanation 
robustness, introducing the concept of self -explicitness and 
demonstrating that SHAP consistently outperforms LIME on 
stability metrics across multiple tabular datasets. [36] 
examined organisational practices surrounding XAI 
deployment in industry, finding that explanation method 
selection was rarely driven by empirical comparative 
evaluation but rather by familiarity and implementation 
convenience—a finding that motivates the rigorous, evidence-
based comparison undertaken in the present study. Most 
recently, benchmarked SHAP, LIME, and several additional 
explanation methods on clinical tabular datasets, reporting that 
SHAP achieved superior faithfulness whilst LIME exhibited 
higher computational efficiency —a trade -off with direct 
practical relevance for real -time ICU monitoring applications  
[37]. 
 
Evaluation Frameworks for Clinical XAI 
The absence of standardised evaluation frameworks for 
clinical XAI has been widely identified as a critical barrier to 
the systematic advancement of the field [22]. Doshi-Velez and

Kim [9] provided an influential tripartite taxonomy of XAI 
evaluation approaches distinguishing application -grounded 
evaluation (assessment by domain experts in realistic task 
contexts), human -grounded evaluation (simplified human 
subject studies), and fun ctionally grounded evaluation (proxy 
metrics requiring no human subjects). [38] proposed the 
RemOve And Retrain (ROAR) benchmark as a functionally 
grounded faithfulness metric, demonstrating that commonly 
used gradient-based explanation methods performed poorly on 
this criterion despite producing visually plausible saliency 
maps—a cautionary finding applicable to feature attribution 
methods in clinical contexts. 
 
Conducted a comprehensive analysis of explainability 
requirements specific to clinical AI, identifying six dimensions 
of explanation quality relevant to healthcare: 
comprehensibility, fidelity, accuracy, scalability, 
generalisability, and causal interpretab ility. Their analysis 
emphasised that clinical explanation requirements differ 
systematically from general XAI requirements, with clinicians 
placing greater emphasis on causal plausibility and 
actionability than on mathematical faithfulness properties [39]. 
[40] raised a particularly sobering concern for clinical XAI, 
demonstrating that COVID -19 chest X -ray classifiers 
achieving high predictive accuracy relied on spurious dataset 
artefacts rather than genuine pathological features —artefacts 
that SHAP and Grad-CAM explanations failed to consistently 
detect—underscoring the necessity of rigorous, multi -criteria 
explanation evaluation rather than reliance on any single 
explanation quality metric. 
 
3. Dataset, Cohort Selection, and Feature 
Engineering  
 
3.1 Dataset Overview: MIMIC-IV 
All experiments reported in this paper utilise the Medical 
Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, 
a large, freely accessible, de -identified critical care database 
developed and maintained by the Laboratory for 
Computational Physiology at the Massachusetts Institute of 
Technology [6]. MIMIC -IV encompasses clinical data from 
adult patients admitted to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical 
Centre (BIDMC) in Boston, Massachusetts, between 2008 and 
2019, representing a substantial expansion in b oth temporal 
coverage and data richness relative to its predecessor, MIMIC-
III. The database comprises several interconnected modules, 
including the hospital module (hosp), which contains 
admission records, laboratory results, microbiology cultures, 
medication administrations, and discharge diagnoses coded 
according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-
9 and ICD-10); and the ICU module (icu), which contains high-
resolution time -series measurements of vital signs, ventilator 
settings, fluid i nputs and outputs, and nurse -documented 
clinical assessments charted at the bedside [6]. Access to 
MIMIC-IV is governed by a data use agreement administered 
through the PhysioNet platform [41], requiring completion of 
a certified human subjects research tr aining programme prior 
to data access authorisation. 
 
MIMIC-IV represents a methodologically superior foundation 
for ICU mortality prediction research relative to predecessor 
datasets for several reasons. First, its substantially larger 
patient population —encompassing over 500,000 hospital 
admissions and appr oximately 76,000 ICU stays —provides 
greater statistical power for subgroup analyses and supports 
more robust evaluation of explanation consistency across 
diverse patient presentations [6]. Second, the temporal span of 
the database (2008 –2019) enables inves tigation of temporal 
distribution shift in both patient characteristics and clinical 
coding practices—a phenomenon with direct implications for 
the generalisability of trained models and the stability of 
generated explanations. Third, MIMIC -IV's modular 
architecture, with clearly delineated hospital and ICU data 
modules linked through consistent patient and admission 
identifiers, facilitates reproducible cohort construction and 
feature engineering pipelines. 
 
3.2 Cohort Selection 
Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria 
The study cohort is constructed from all adult ICU admissions 
recorded in the MIMIC-IV ICU module. Inclusion criteria are: 
(i) patient age ≥ 18 years at time of ICU admission; (ii) ICU 
length of stay ≥ 24 hours, ensuring sufficient observation time 
for feature extraction across the prediction window; and (iii) 
availability of a minimum of 60% of required clinical features 
within the first 24 hours of ICU admission, consistent with 
methodological standards established in prior MIMIC -based 
benchmarking studie s [7]. Exclusion criteria are: (i) patients 
with a recorded ICU length of stay < 24 hours, as mortality 
predictions for this subgroup reflect acute catastrophic 
deterioration rather than the progressive physiological 
derangements targeted by the model; (ii ) repeat ICU 
admissions within the same hospital encounter, retaining only 
the first ICU stay per hospital admission to avoid outcome 
correlation between observations; and (iii) patients aged < 18 
years, as paediatric critical illness pathophysiology and 
mortality determinants differ fundamentally from adult 
populations. 
 
Outcome Definition 
The primary prediction target is in -hospital mortality, defined 
as a binary indicator variable equal to one if the patient died 
during the index hospital admission and zero otherwise, 
regardless of discharge destination. In -hospital mortality is 
selected o ver ICU mortality as the primary outcome on the 
grounds that it captures deaths occurring after ICU discharge 
but prior to hospital discharge —a clinically important 
population whose risk is substantially determined by the 
severity of physiological derangement during the ICU stay [2]. 
Secondary prediction targets—including 24-hour and 48-hour 
ICU mortality—are evaluated to assess the temporal stability 
of explanation patterns across prediction horizons. 
 
Cohort Construction Pipeline 
Cohort construction is implemented as a reproducible Python 
pipeline utilising structured query language (SQL) queries 
executed against a local PostgreSQL instance of MIMIC -IV, 
following the methodology established by the MIMIC-Extract 
framework [42]. The pipeline proceeds through four sequential

Time-Series Feature Extraction 
Vital sign and laboratory time -series data are extracted from 
the chartevents and labevents tables of MIMIC -IV 
respectively, filtered to the first 24 hours of each patient's ICU 
admission. For each continuous physiological variable, six 
summary statistics are computed over the 24-hour observation 
window: minimum, maximum, mean, first recorded value, last 
recorded value, and range (maximum minus minimum). This 
approach follows established practice in MIMIC -based 
benchmarking [7] and produces features directl y interpretable 
within clinical reasoning frameworks—for instance, minimum 
mean arterial pressure (MAP) over 24 hours reflects the nadir 
of haemodynamic stability, whilst the range of lactate reflects 
the trajectory of tissue perfusion rather than a single time-point 
snapshot. 
 
Laboratory values are extracted from the labevents table, with 
fluid-of-measurement standardisation applied to resolve 
discrepancies arising from different laboratory analysers and 
reporting conventions across the MIMIC-IV time span. Where 
multiple measure ments of the same variable exist within the 
24-hour window, all values are retained for aggregate statistic 
computation. Item IDs for each laboratory test are specified 
using the standardised MIMIC -IV item dictionaries (d_items 
and d_labitems), with cross -referencing against published 
MIMIC item ID lists [42] to ensure correct variable 
identification. 
 
Severity Score Computation 
SOFA scores are computed from constituent organ system 
components—respiratory (PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio), coagulation 
(platelet count), hepatic (bilirubin), cardiovascular (MAP and 
vasopressor use), neurological (Glasgow Coma Scale), and 
renal (creatinine and urine output)—extracted from the first 24 
hours of ICU admission and aggregated according to the 
original SOFA scoring algorithm [3]. Where component 
variables required for SOFA computation are unavailable, 
item-specific imputation strategies are applied. APACH E III 
component scores are extracted directly from the 
apachepatientresult table available in MIMIC -IV, which 
contains pre -computed severity scores for a subset of 
admissions, supplemented by manual computation from raw 
physiological data for admissions no t covered by the pre -
computed table. 
 
Clinical Intervention Features 
Binary indicator variables are constructed for three clinical 
interventions documented within the first 24 hours of ICU 
admission: (i) mechanical ventilation, identified through the 
presence of ventilator-related item IDs in the chartevents table 
or proced ureevents table; (ii) vasopressor administration, 
identified through the presence of norepinephrine, epinephrine, 
dopamine, vasopressin, or phenylephrine infusion records in 
the inputevents table; and (iii) continuous renal replacement 
therapy (CRRT), iden tified through procedure event records. 
These intervention flags capture a clinically important 
dimension of illness severity that is not fully reflected in 
physiological time-series data alone, as the initiation of organ 
support therapies is itself a mark er of critical physiological 
derangement [4]. 
 
Missing Data Handling 
Missing data represents a pervasive challenge in EHR -based 
clinical prediction research, arising from the opportunistic 
rather than protocol-driven nature of clinical data collection in 
the ICU [43]. A structured missingness handling strategy is 
applied, c omprising three tiers based on missingness 
mechanism and clinical context. For features with missingness 
rate < 10% under a plausible missing -at-random (MAR) 
assumption—principally demographic variables and routinely 
collected vital signs —multiple imputati on by chained 
equations (MICE) is applied using the implementation 
available in the scikit -learn IterativeImputer class [44]. For 
laboratory variables with missingness rates between 10% and 
40%, median imputation stratified by primary admission 
diagnosis c ategory is applied, consistent with the approach 
adopted by Harutyunyan et al. [7]. For variables with 
missingness rates exceeding 40% —identified in preliminary 
analysis as including arterial blood gas parameters in non -
ventilated patients —missingness indi cator variables are 
appended to the feature matrix alongside median -imputed 
values, enabling gradient boosting models to learn differential 
behaviour conditioned on data availability [27].

Feature Preprocessing and Normalisation 
Continuous features are standardised to zero mean and unit 
variance using parameters estimated on the training set, 
consistent with the requirements of LIME's linear surrogate 
model fitting procedure. Binary and categorical features are 
retained in their original encoding. Outlier values arising from 
data entry errors are identified using physiologically 
implausible value thresholds defined in consultation with 
clinical literature [3], [4] and replaced with missing values 
prior to imputation. Age values are  top-coded at 91 years in 
MIMIC-IV in accordance with patient de -identification 
requirements and  are retained as such without further 
transformation. 
 
3.4 Dataset Splitting and Class Imbalance 
The full extracted cohort is partitioned into training (70%), 
validation (15%), and test (15%) sets using stratified random 
sampling to preserve the in-hospital mortality rate across splits. 
Stratification is additionally applied with respect to calendar 
year of admission to mitigate temporal confounding arising 
from changes in clinical practice and data collection 
procedures across the MIMIC-IV time span. 
 
Class imbalance—reflecting the natural epidemiology of ICU 
mortality, where survivors substantially outnumber non -
survivors—is addressed through a combination of class -
weighted loss functions during model training and synthetic 
minority oversampling (SMOTE ) [45] applied exclusively to 
the training set. The application of SMOTE is restricted to the 
training partition to prevent the introduction of synthetic 
observations into evaluation sets, which would produce 
artificially optimistic performance estimates. 
 
All data extraction SQL queries, Python preprocessing scripts, 
and cohort construction pipelines are implemented following 
FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) data 
principles [46]. MIMIC -IV data itself cannot be redistributed 
in accordance with the PhysioNet data use agreement. Random 
seeds are fixed throughout all stochastic pipeline components 
to ensure full reproducibility of reported results. 
 
4. Predictive Model Implementation and 
Performance Evaluation 
 
4.1 Model Selection Rationale 
Two gradient boosting frameworks are selected as the 
predictive modelling backbone for this study: Extreme 
Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) [47] and Light Gradient 
Boosting Machine (LightGBM) [48]. This selection is 
motivated by four considerations.  
• First, gradient boosting methods consistently achieve 
state-of-the-art predictive performance on tabular EHR 
data, outperforming deep learning alternatives in the 
presence of mixed feature types, moderate dataset sizes, 
and heterogeneous missingness patterns characteristic of 
critical care databases [27].  
• Second, both XGBoost and LightGBM are natively 
compatible with TreeSHAP [21], enabling exact Shapley 
value computation with polynomial rather than 
exponential computational complexity —a property 
essential for the SHAP evaluation.  
• Third, the interpretability literature has most extensively 
validated post -hoc explanation methods on gradient 
boosting models applied to tabular clinical data [20], [25], 
ensuring that the comparative evaluation reported in this 
paper is situated within a n established methodological 
context.  
• Fourth, both frameworks provide native handling of 
missing values through learned default split directions, 
complementing the imputation strategy and enabling 
sensitivity analysis of explanation outputs under different 
missingness assumptions. 
 
Logistic regression with elastic net regularisation is 
additionally implemented as an interpretable baseline model, 
providing a reference point against which the predictive 
performance gains of gradient boosting —and the associated 
explanation complexity co sts—can be quantified. Whilst 
logistic regression coefficients provide a form of intrinsic 
interpretability, their validity as explanations depends on the 
assumption of a correctly specified linear model, an 
assumption unlikely to hold in the presence of t he non-linear 
physiological interactions characteristic of critical illness [11].

4.3 LightGBM Implementation 
Algorithm Overview 
LightGBM implements gradient boosted trees using two key 
algorithmic innovations that distinguish it from XGBoost: 
Gradient-based One -Side Sampling (GOSS) and Exclusive 
Feature Bundling (EFB) [48]. GOSS reduces the effective 
training sample size by retaini ng all instances with large 
gradients—which carry more information for tree 
construction—whilst randomly sampling a proportion of 
instances with small gradients, thereby accelerating training 
without substantial loss of predictive accuracy. EFB reduces 
effective feature dimensionality by bundling mutually 
exclusive sparse features into single composite features, 
exploiting the observation that in high -dimensional clinical 
feature spaces, many features are rarely simultaneously non -
zero. 
 
LightGBM further differs from XGBoost in its tree growth 
strategy, employing leaf -wise rather than level -wise tree 
expansion. Leaf -wise growth selects the leaf with the 
maximum loss reduction for splitting at each iteration, 
producing asymmetric trees that can model complex non-linear 
relationships more efficiently than the symmetric level -wise 
approach—a property potentially advantageous in ICU 
mortality prediction where the relationship between 
physiological variables and mortality exhibits substantial non-
linearity and interaction effects [28].

4.4 Logistic Regression Baseline 
Logistic regression with elastic net regularisation is 
implemented using scikit-learn's LogisticRegression class [44] 
with the saga solver, selected for its computational efficiency 
on large datasets and native support for L1 and L2 penalty 
mixing. The elastic net mixing parameter α and regularisation 
strength C are optimised via grid search over α ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 
0.7, 0.9} and C ∈ {0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0} using 5 -fold 
stratified cross-validation on the training set. Class imbalance 
is addressed thro ugh the class_weight='balanced' parameter, 
which adjusts sample weights inversely proportional to class 
frequencies. 
 
4.5 Model Calibration 
Probability calibration is applied to all three models using Platt 
scaling [50], implemented through scikit -learn's 
CalibratedClassifierCV with the sigmoid method fitted on the 
validation set. Calibration is assessed using the Expected 
Calibration Error (E CE) and the Brier Score, with reliability 
diagrams generated by partitioning predicted probability 
outputs into ten equally spaced bins and plotting mean 
predicted probability against observed mortality rate within 
each bin. Well-calibrated mortality risk estimates are a clinical 
prerequisite for meaningful explanation, as SHAP baseline 
values and LIME local approximations are interpretable 
relative to model output probabilities —if these probabilities 
are systematically miscalibrated, the clinical interpret ation of 
feature attributions is correspondingly compromised. 
 
4.6 Performance Evaluation Metrics 
Model performance is evaluated on the held -out test set using 
six metrics spanning discrimination, calibration, and clinical 
utility dimensions: 
1)  AUROC: Primary discrimination metric; area under the 
receiver operating characteristic curve

3)  Brier Score: Composite measure of calibration and

4)  Expected Calibration Error (ECE): Mean absolute

5.1 Implementation Architecture 
The explanation framework implements two post -hoc, model-
agnostic explanation methods—SHAP and LIME—applied to 
both XGBoost and LightGBM mortality prediction models.

returning standardised explanation objects compatible with the 
evaluation framework. This unified architecture (Figure 1)

The comparative evaluation of TreeSHAP , ensemble LIME, 
and Anchors across three explanation quality dimensions 
reveals a nuanced performance landscape in which no single 
method achieves uniform superiority across all criteria. 
TreeSHAP demonstrates the strongest overall performance, 
achieving the highest faithfulness scores (mean AOPC: 0.276; 
sufficiency at K=5: 0.821) and the greatest stability (Lipschitz 
estimate: 0.312), reflecting the axiomatic guarantees of 
Shapley values and the deterministic nature of the TreeSHAP 
algorithm, which elimin ates stochastic variability entirely. 
Ensemble LIME occupies an intermediate position: its 
faithfulness (mean AOPC: 0.201; sufficiency at K=5: 0.743) 
and stability (Lipschitz estimate: 0.614; run variance CV: 
0.094) are substantially inferior to TreeSHAP b ut represent a 
meaningful improvement over single -run LIME, with the 
ensemble averaging procedure reducing coefficient of 
variation from 0.412 to 0.094 at the cost of a 30 -fold increase 
in computation time. Anchors achieve the lowest faithfulness 
scores ac ross all perturbation steps (mean AOPC: 0.148; 
sufficiency at K=5: 0.681) and exhibit intermediate stability 
(Lipschitz estimate: 0.941; precision CV: 0.127), reflecting the 
inherent limitations of rule-based explanations in capturing the 
continuous, high-dimensional decision boundaries of gradient 
boosting models applied to heterogeneous ICU patient 
populations. However, Anchors demonstrate a decisive 
comprehensibility advantage: their mean rule length of 3.84 
conjunctive predicates produces compact, directly readable if-
then statements requiring no statistical interpretation, 
compared with TreeSHAP's full 64 -feature attribution 
decomposition (entropy: 3.21) and ensemble LIME's 20 -
feature surrogate model (entropy: 2.47). These findings 
collectively suggest that TreeSHAP is most appropriate as the 
primary explanation mechanism for real -time clinical 
deployment where faithfulness and stability are paramount, 
whilst Anchors serve a complementary role in generating 
concise rule -based summaries for non -technical stakeholders 
and governance contexts where comprehensibility takes 
precedence over mathematical precision. 
 
Limitations of the Study 
Three principal limitations should be acknowledged.  
• First, the evaluation is conducted on a single institution's 
data (MIMIC-IV, Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Centre), 
and generalisation of explanation quality findings to other

healthcare systems and patient populations remains 
unvalidated [29].  
• Second, the feature engineering pipeline is restricted to 
structured EHR variables over the first 24 hours of ICU 
admission, excluding unstructured clinical notes, imaging 
data, and longer temporal trajectories that may carry 
additional prognostic information.  
• Third, the evaluation relies on quantitative proxy metrics 
and expert plausibility ratings rather than prospective 
assessment of whether explanations improve clinical 
decision quality in live workflows —the most clinically 
meaningful evidence standard. 
 
7. Conclusion and Future Scope 
 
This paper has presented a rigorous technical investigation of 
two widely adopted post -hoc explanation methods —SHAP, 
LIME and Anchors —implemented and systematically 
compared on XGBoost and LightGBM mortality prediction 
models trained on the MIMIC -IV critical care database. The 
study makes three principal contributions. First, complete, 
reproducible implementations of TreeSHAP , LIME and 
Anchors are provided within a unified explanation pipeline 
applied to a large -scale, contemporary ICU dataset 
encompassing 48,372 admissions and 64 clinical features. 
Second, a multi -criteria evaluation framework assessing 
faithfulness, stability and comprehensibility is applied, 
providing a structured methodology for explanation quality 
assessment reusable in future clinical XAI studies. Third, 
rigorous head-to-head empirical comparison of SHAP , LIME 
and Anchors  is conducted under identical experimental 
conditions, addressing a gap in the literature identified by 
Payrovnaziri et al. [23] and Markus et al. [22], where 
comparative multi -criteria evaluation on large -scale clinical 
datasets is notably absent.  
 
Future investigations should evaluate the framework on 
external critical care datasets to assess generalisability, extend 
the feature set to multimodal and longitudinal clinical data, and 
conduct prospective trials measuring the impact of 
explanation-enhanced predictions on clinician decision quality 
and patient outcomes. Integration of causal inference methods 
with SHAP —addressing the well -documented limitations of 
associational feature attribution under feature correlation 
[53]—represents a particularly p romising direction for 
enhancing the clinical actionability of generated explanations.

2024-25’, and listed in ‘100 Eminent Academicians of 2021’
